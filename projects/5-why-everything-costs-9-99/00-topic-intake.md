## Raw Idea

Almost every price ends in `.99`. $9.99 is one cent away from $10, yet it feels meaningfully
cheaper — every time, even when you know the trick. Same family as the channel's
`why-buy-1-get-1-beats-50-off` video: a tiny pricing detail that hijacks one mental shortcut and
makes the shopper the rabbit.

## Clean Angle

`$9.99 is not "basically $10." Your brain reads a price left-to-right and anchors on the first
digit, so it files $9.99 under "nine-something" and quietly forgets the cents. One missing penny
rewrites the price your brain remembers — the cost barely changes, but the feeling drops by almost
a whole dollar.`

Sentence test:
`This video is about how $9.99 looks like a real discount, but is actually the same price your brain
just refuses to read correctly.`

## Viewer Curiosity

`Wait, that's true — I KNOW it's basically $10, so why does $9.99 still feel cheaper?`

The curiosity gap is not "prices end in 99" (everyone has noticed). It is the mechanism: *why it
still works on you after you know about it.*

## Must Stay Honest (guardrail for research + script)

This is a perception nudge, not a lie or a math scam. Bake these conditions into research and script
so the video stays trustworthy (same discipline as the BOGO honesty section):

- The dominant, learner-clean explanation is the **left-digit effect**: people encode price
  magnitude from the leftmost digit, so $9.99 reads as "9-something" and the perceived gap vs $10
  is closer to ~$1 than to the real $0.01. Verify with a real source in `research-pack` (academic
  pricing research / reputable explainer); do not overstate exact figures.
- Secondary, related mechanisms to mention lightly, not as the whole story: a price ending in `9`
  also **signals "deal/discount"**, and "just-below" pricing is partly habit/convention. Keep
  left-digit as the spine.
- It is a **tendency, not a law**: the effect is small per item and context-dependent. Avoid
  "this always works / everyone falls for it 100%."
- It **reverses at the premium end**: luxury / high-status brands deliberately use **round numbers**
  ($100, $1,000) because round prices feel more confident and premium. This is the video's honest
  turn (like BOGO's loss-leader section) — ".99 feels cheap" is exactly why expensive things avoid it.
- The store is not hiding the real price; the full price is printed. The "trick" is that your brain
  under-reads it. Keep the roast aimed at the *trick* and the *viewer's own brain*, never a shop,
  brand, or group.

## Main Risk

The fact is so familiar people assume they already understand it. Mitigation: the hook must promise
the **mechanism** ("why one cent still fools you after you know the trick"), not the fact; and the
**premium-reversal turn** ($100 luxury prices) keeps the back half surprising. Keep it honest — a
perception nudge, not a "stores are lying" rant.
