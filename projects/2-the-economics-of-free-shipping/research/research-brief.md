# Research brief - The Economics of Free Shipping

## Central question

If shipping is one of the largest real costs in online retail, who is actually paying for it when the checkout screen says the delivery is free?

## Familiar moment

Your cart holds one item you want. The page says you are a few dollars short of free shipping. You go back to the catalog, find something you were not going to buy, and add it. The shipping line changes to $0.00 and the order feels solved.

## Common belief

Free shipping is a discount. The retailer absorbs the delivery cost as a marketing expense, so the shopper who qualifies gets the same product for less money.

## Contradiction

Delivery is a physical cost that does not disappear when it stops being displayed. Amazon reported $102.7 billion of shipping costs in 2025, up from $95.8 billion in 2024, and books those costs inside cost of sales rather than against a separate shipping revenue line. The same filing states that offering lower prices through shipping offers is a deliberate strategy. The fee is removed from the invoice, not from the economy of the order. [C01, C02, C03, S01]

## One-sentence reframe

Free shipping is not a discount, it is a relocation. The delivery cost moves out of a line you can see and into the product price, the basket size, the membership fee, and the fees the seller pays, where you can no longer price it.

## Unit economics

Illustration, not a market average. You want a $32 item from a store with a $6.99 flat shipping fee and a $50 free-shipping threshold.

Pay the fee: $32.00 + $6.99 = $38.99 total, and you own only the item you wanted.

Reach the threshold: add an $18.00 item, and $32.00 + $18.00 = $50.00 total with no shipping line.

Avoiding the $6.99 fee cost you $11.01 more. The arithmetic is $50.00 - $38.99 = $11.01, which is the same as $18.00 - $6.99 = $11.01. The delivery still happened and was still paid for. What changed is that you paid for it by buying more, and the receipt calls the shipping free. [C11]

On the retailer side of the same transaction, Amazon's 2025 shipping costs of $102.7 billion were roughly 2.1 times its $49.6 billion of subscription services net sales, which is the line that contains Prime membership fees along with digital content subscriptions. The membership fee alone does not fund the delivery network. [C02, C04, C12, S01]

## Incentive map

| Actor | Pays | Receives | Controls | Incentive |
| ----- | ---- | -------- | -------- | --------- |
| Shopper | Product price, larger basket, membership fee, return effort | Delivery with no visible fee | Whether to add an item to cross a threshold | Avoid a fee that feels like a loss and reach a round number that feels solved |
| Retailer | Real carrier, sortation, and delivery costs | Higher order value and repeat purchase | Threshold level, fee schedule, what appears on the checkout line | Convert a fee that causes abandonment into a basket that raises revenue |
| Marketplace seller | Platform fees, packaging, and shipping inputs | Placement and access to buyers | Item price, which must absorb shipping to stay competitive | Keep the displayed price and the shipping line attractive at the same time |
| Carrier and delivery worker | Labor, vehicles, time density | Per-parcel payment | Route and delivery speed, not the retail offer | Move more parcels per stop as free-shipping volume grows |
| Regulator | Enforcement | Compliance with price-disclosure rules | The legal definition of a shipping charge and what may be excluded from an advertised total price | Stop hidden fees without banning true cost pass-through |
| Non-participating shopper | The same product price | Nothing extra | Nothing about the threshold | Bears part of a delivery cost embedded in prices even when buying in a store |

## Behavioral engine

Two mechanisms stack, and neither requires the shopper to be careless.

The first is the zero-price effect. Shampanier, Mazar, and Ariely showed across hypothetical and real-purchase experiments that a price of zero does not behave like a very small price. In a field experiment with 398 participants at an MIT student center, moving a Hershey's Kiss from 1 cent to 0 cents while cutting the Lindt truffle from 15 cents to 14 cents raised the Hershey's share from 14% to 42% and cut the Lindt share from 36% to 19%. When people who bought nothing were removed, the Hershey's share went from 27% to 69% and Lindt fell from 73% to 31%. A follow-up cafeteria experiment with 232 customers held transaction costs constant and reproduced the pattern. The authors conclude that affect, not social norms or difficulty translating value into money, best explains the effect. These are small-stakes candy choices, which is a real limit on generalization, and the authors say so. [C05, C06, C07, S03]

The second is the threshold. Lewis, Singh, and Fay analyzed an online grocery and drugstore retailer that varied its shipping fee schedules, and found that shipping charges strongly affect both whether an order happens and how large it is, with threshold-based free shipping effective at generating sales. That is the mechanism that turns a shortfall message into an added item. [C08, S04]

Naming the mechanism precisely matters. The shopper is not confused about arithmetic. The shopper is responding to a price of zero as a different category of thing, inside a checkout that has been designed to place a threshold just above where the basket currently sits.

## Hidden system and mid-video reveal

The mid-video reveal is that United States regulators drew this exact line themselves, and drew it in the opposite direction from the intuition that shipping fees are junk fees.

The FTC Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees, published at 90 FR 2166 on January 10, 2025 and effective May 12, 2025, targets hidden and misleading fees in live-event ticketing and short-term lodging. It defines a shipping charge as a fee that reasonably reflects the amount a business incurs to send physical goods to a consumer, and it allows shipping charges, government charges, and optional add-on fees to be excluded from the advertised total price. The rulemaking record states that the rule gives businesses discretion to list government and shipping charges separately. [C09, C10, S02, S05]

So in the one place the federal government defined a deceptive fee, it treated a shipping charge as the opposite: a real cost being passed through. That reframes the whole category. A visible shipping fee is the honest version. Free shipping is the version where the same real cost has been moved somewhere you cannot compare.

The second half of the reveal is that the relocation feeds back. Lepthien and Clement ran a randomized field experiment testing seven shipping fee schedules, varying minimum order sizes, fees, and free-shipping thresholds, and tracked purchases and returns together. Threshold-based free shipping generated more strategic returns across all customers while reducing purchase frequency overall. The threshold does not only change what enters the cart. It changes what comes back. [C13, S06]

## Case study

Amazon is the clearest case because it publishes the numbers.

Shipping costs were $95.8 billion in 2024 and $102.7 billion in 2025, an increase of $6.9 billion or about 7.2%. Those costs are recorded inside cost of sales, alongside the purchase price of the goods, rather than being offset against a disclosed shipping revenue line. The company writes that it expects shipping cost to keep rising to the extent customers accept and use its shipping offers at an increasing rate, and that one way it offers lower prices is through shipping offers. [C01, C02, C03, S01]

The funding is visible in the revenue mix. In 2025, subscription services net sales, which include Prime memberships, were $49.6 billion. Advertising services were $68.6 billion and third-party seller services were $172.2 billion, against total net sales of $716.9 billion. US Prime is listed at $14.99 per month or $139 per year, with lower-priced student and government-assistance tiers. The delivery promise is not paid for by the delivery line, and it is not paid for by the membership fee alone. It is carried by the whole apparatus of product margin, seller fees, and advertising. [C04, C12, C14, C15, S01, S07]

Amazon's own history supplies the behavioral half. Shampanier, Mazar, and Ariely report that when Amazon introduced free shipping in several European countries, the price in France was set not to zero but to one French franc, roughly ten cents, and that order volumes rose sharply in the free-shipping countries while France saw little change. The authors present this explicitly as anecdotal evidence, not as a measured result, and it should be told that way. [C16, S03]

## Counterargument and boundary conditions

Free shipping is not only a trick, and the strongest counterargument is that consolidation is genuinely efficient. One larger parcel usually costs less to move than several small ones, so a threshold that produces bigger, less frequent orders can lower the true delivery cost per item. That is a real economy, not an illusion.

The visible fee is also a real cost to shoppers, not merely a psychological one. Baymard Institute reports that among US online shoppers, once the large "just browsing" group is set aside, extra costs such as shipping, tax, and fees are the leading reason for abandoning a cart at 40%, ahead of slow delivery at 20%. Removing that friction has value. [C17, S08]

And the retailer is not a guaranteed winner. Lewis, Singh, and Fay found that free-shipping promotions and thresholds are effective at generating sales but often reduce profitability, because of forgone shipping revenue and uneven uptake across customer segments. Lepthien and Clement found threshold free shipping cut purchase frequency. This is a system with costs on both sides of the counter, not a one-way extraction. [C08, C13, S04, S06]

Two limits on scope. The FTC rule covers live-event tickets and short-term lodging, not general retail, so it defines the concepts without governing your cart. And the Amazon figures are one company's global consolidated accounts, which include inbound freight and paid shipping, so they establish the scale of the cost, not the price of any single free delivery. [C09, C01]

## Human price

The money is the smallest part. In the illustration you spent $11.01 more to avoid $6.99, and the pattern repeats every time a threshold sits a little above your basket.

The larger prices are these. You do arithmetic at every checkout, deciding whether a delivery fee is fair without knowing what delivery costs, which is the friction 40% of abandoning shoppers name. You hold more goods than you chose, and hand more of them back, because threshold free shipping produces more strategic returns. Across the retail industry, returns were projected to reach $890 billion in 2024, about 16.9% of annual sales, in a market where 76% of surveyed consumers treat free returns as a key factor in where they shop, which means the reverse leg of the same promise is now a permanent cost inside prices. And you lose the ability to compare, because once the delivery cost has been folded into the product price, no shopper, including the ones who never use the free shipping, can see what they are paying for it.

That last sentence is an inference from the filing treatment of shipping costs, the regulatory definition of a shipping charge, and the threshold experiments, not a measured figure. [C11, C13, C17, C18, C19, S01, S02, S06, S07, S08]

## Title and thumbnail number clearance

No exact number is cleared for the title or the thumbnail.

- $102.7 billion is verified from Amazon's FY2025 10-K but is global, consolidated, and includes inbound freight and paid shipping. Compressed into packaging it would read as the price of free shipping, which the source does not support. Rejected for title and thumbnail. Cleared for the script with the scope stated.
- $139 is a verified first-party US Prime list price, but it is a subscription price, not a shipping price, and a title built on it would promise a Prime episode. Rejected for title and thumbnail. Cleared for the script.
- $890 billion and 16.9% are survey-based industry projections about returns across all retail, not about shipping. Rejected for title and thumbnail. Cleared for the script as returns context with the method stated.
- The $11.01 figure is an illustration built from invented inputs. Never eligible for packaging.

Use a contradiction title: `The Economics of Free Shipping: Why Free Is the Most Expensive`. The thumbnail may show a zeroed shipping line, since $0.00 is a rendering of the checkout, not a factual claim.
