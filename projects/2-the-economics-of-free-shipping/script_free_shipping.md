Your cart has one item in it. Thirty-two dollars. Shipping, six ninety-nine.

Then the store tells you something helpful. Spend fifty, and shipping is free.

So you go back. You find something for eighteen dollars. You are not certain you want it. You add it anyway. The shipping line turns to zero.

You have just spent eleven dollars and one cent more than if you had paid the fee.

Not less. More.

And the checkout was not lying to you. That is the strange part.

The ordinary explanation is that free shipping is a discount. The store absorbs the delivery cost to win your business, the way it marks down a sweater.

But delivery is not a marketing idea. It is a truck, a driver, a sorting building, and a pair of hands. That cost does not vanish when it stops being printed. Free shipping is not a discount. It is a relocation. The cost moves out of a line you can see and into places you cannot price. The product. The size of your basket. The membership fee. The fees a seller pays to be in front of you.

Do that arithmetic slowly.

Option one. You buy the thirty-two dollar item and pay six ninety-nine. Thirty-eight ninety-nine leaves your account, and you own exactly what you came for.

Option two. You add the eighteen dollar item. The order is fifty dollars and the shipping line reads free. Fifty dollars leaves your account.

Fifty minus thirty-eight ninety-nine is eleven dollars and one cent. The other way around, the extra item cost eighteen dollars and saved you six ninety-nine. Same answer.

Those numbers are an illustration, not a market average. But the shape is the point. To make a seven dollar fee disappear, you spent eighteen dollars. The van still came. The driver still walked to your door. Somebody paid for all of it. The payment is simply no longer labeled.

Look at who is in the room. Everyone here is behaving sensibly.

You want the delivery, and you would rather not be charged for something you cannot shop around.

The retailer knows the fee is where orders go to die, and that a threshold turns a fee into a bigger basket.

A marketplace seller must keep the item price and the shipping line attractive at once, so delivery goes into the price of the thing.

The carrier and the driver are paid per parcel and per stop, and have no say in any of it.

And then there is someone who never shops online, who walks into a store and pays a price with a share of this system baked in. They never see the door.

The engine is not that you are bad at arithmetic. It is that zero is not a number your mind treats like other numbers.

In a study published in Marketing Science, researchers set up a stand at MIT and offered two chocolates. A Hershey's Kiss for one cent, a Lindt truffle for fifteen. Then they cut both prices by exactly one penny. The Lindt went to fourteen. The Hershey's went to zero.

Nothing about the relative deal changed. Both got a penny cheaper. But the share taking the Hershey's went from fourteen percent to forty-two, and the Lindt fell from thirty-six to nineteen. Three hundred and ninety-eight people. Real money, real chocolate. They ran it again in a cafeteria, adding the cost to a bill people were already paying so every choice carried identical hassle. Same result.

This is candy. Small sums, small stakes, and the authors say plainly that this limits how far it should be stretched. But a price of zero does not behave like a low price. It behaves like a different category of thing, and the researchers concluded the best explanation is how a free offer feels, not what it costs.

Put that beside the second mechanism. A separate study of an online retailer that kept changing its shipping fee schedules found that shipping charges strongly affect both whether you order and how much you put in the basket, and that thresholds are very effective at generating sales. The threshold does not have to persuade you of anything. It only has to sit a little above where your cart already is.

So where does the money go. Look at the largest version of this system, which publishes its accounts.

In its most recent annual report, Amazon put shipping costs at one hundred and two point seven billion dollars, up from ninety-five point eight. That number is global and covers goods moving in as well as out, so it is not a price tag for free delivery. It is the size of the machine.

Here is the detail that matters. Those costs sit inside cost of sales, next to what the company paid for the goods. There is no offsetting shipping revenue line. The company also writes, in that same filing, that it expects shipping costs to keep rising as customers use its shipping offers more, and that offering lower prices through shipping offers is one of the ways it competes.

The cost is not hidden in any improper sense. It is disclosed, in public, to investors. It is hidden from you, at the moment you are deciding.

And now the part that changes the category.

In early two thousand twenty-five, the Federal Trade Commission published a rule on unfair and deceptive fees, effective that May. It is the junk fee rule, aimed at charges that surface late in a purchase. It covers live event tickets and short-term lodging, and nothing else, so it does not govern your cart.

But it had to define its terms, and in doing so the United States government wrote down what a shipping charge is. A shipping charge, it says, is a fee that reasonably reflects the amount a business incurs to send physical goods to a consumer. Because it reflects a real cost, businesses may leave it out of the advertised total price and list it separately.

In the one place the federal government sat down to define a deceptive fee, it classified shipping as the opposite. Not a junk fee. A true cost, passed through. Which means the visible shipping fee was the honest version. Free shipping is the version where that cost still exists and you have lost the ability to see it, compare it, or shop for it.

Then the loop closes. A randomized field experiment at an online retailer tested seven shipping fee schedules and tracked not only what people bought but what they sent back. Free shipping above a threshold produced more strategic returns across all customers, and reduced how often people ordered at all. The threshold does not only decide what goes into the box. It decides what comes back out.

Scale it up and the funding becomes visible. Against that hundred and two billion dollar shipping bill, Amazon's subscription line, which carries Prime memberships along with video and music and books, came to forty-nine point six billion. Less than half. Advertising was sixty-eight point six billion, and third party seller services, the fees merchants pay for access, were a hundred and seventy-two point two billion, out of about seven hundred and seventeen billion in total sales. Prime in the United States lists at fourteen ninety-nine a month, or a hundred and thirty-nine a year.

So the delivery promise is not funded by the delivery fee, and not by your membership either. It is carried by the margin on goods, by what sellers pay for access, and by advertising. All of it reaches you through a price with no line item.

The same researchers tell one more story. When Amazon introduced free shipping across parts of Europe, the price in France was set not to zero but to one franc, about ten cents. Orders jumped in the free shipping countries. In France, they say, not much happened. They offer it as an anecdote, not data, so hold it loosely. But ten cents and nothing are nearly the same number, and they did not behave the same way.

The honest counterargument is that none of this is a trick.

Shipping one large box really is cheaper than shipping three small ones, so a threshold that makes orders bigger and less frequent can genuinely lower the cost of moving each item. That is a real efficiency, not an illusion.

The fee really is a wall, too. In a survey of American online shoppers, once you set aside everyone who was only browsing, the leading reason for abandoning a cart was extra costs like shipping, tax, and fees. Forty percent. Removing that friction is worth something to shoppers too.

And the retailer is not simply winning. The research that found thresholds generate sales also found they often reduce profitability, because of the shipping revenue given up and because uptake is uneven.

Run the stress test. Suppose every online price had to be shown delivered. One number, no shipping line at all.

Small orders would become visibly more expensive. Large orders would get cheaper. Thresholds would lose their grip, because there would be nothing left to zero out. And you could compare two stores on a single number for the first time in years. Whether total spending would fall, the evidence here does not say.

Which leaves the human price, and it is not mainly the eleven dollars.

You pay in arithmetic. Every checkout asks you to judge whether a delivery charge is fair without ever telling you what delivery costs. Forty percent of abandoned carts is what that unanswerable question looks like at scale.

You pay in objects. You hold more things than you chose, and you hand more of them back. Across American retail, returns were projected at eight hundred and ninety billion dollars in one recent year, about seventeen percent of sales, in a market where more than three quarters of shoppers say free returns decide where they shop. The return trip is a permanent cost inside prices.

And you pay in blindness, which is the expensive one. Once the cost of delivery has been folded into the price of the goods, nobody can see it. Not you. Not the shopper who never uses free shipping and pays for it anyway. Not the person comparing two stores.

Go back to the cart. Eleven dollars and one cent, and a shipping line that reads free.

It was true. Shipping was free.

The delivery was not.
