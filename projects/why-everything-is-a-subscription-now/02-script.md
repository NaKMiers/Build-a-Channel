## Draft Strategy

- Core thesis: Companies rebuilt modern life around renting access instead of selling ownership, because a customer who pays a little every month is worth far more than a one-time buyer — and the real product became *you not cancelling*.
- Recurring motif (NEW): **screens that own you.** Every device WIT has — phone, laptop, monitor, TV, smartwatch, car screen — glows, fills with subscription rows, and slams a padlock over itself the second a payment stops. The cancel "maze" is a phone-menu maze. The one screen that never asks for money is the bank app showing his paycheck.
- WIT arc: suspicious of a mystery charge → ambushed by his own devices → outmatched by the money machine → tricked by a free trial → trapped in a phone-menu maze → quietly turning the phone back against the system.
- Humor: dry but denser than rev 1. See "Humor System" below.
- Script risk: (1) drifting into "subscriptions evil, cancel everything" preaching; (2) trendy jokes aging fast or losing English learners; (3) a brand example with an unverified figure. Mitigation: keep the system happening *to* WIT, say plainly twice that some subscriptions are worth it, pair every trending phrase with an on-screen visual so it reads from context, and keep examples generic/soft.

## Section 1: Hook: It's More Than You Think

Estimated time: `0:00-0:20`

Words: `75`

Purpose: Open a curiosity gap fast, in short punchy lines that sound spoken (not read), and pay the title promise by second 10.

Visual goal: Open straight on a phone's bank-app screen — a list of small monthly charges scrolling past, one circled in red with a glowing `?`. WIT leans in and squints, suspicious. The pop-up `Your free trial of owning things just expired` lands. In the background, his laptop, TV, watch, and car screen quietly flick on, each with its own tiny charge — teasing the rest of the video.

Narration:

```text
Quick question. How many subscriptions are you paying for right now?

[beat] Whatever number you guessed... it's higher. It's always higher.

Right now, money is quietly leaving your account every month — for stuff you forgot you own. An app. A "free" trial that stopped being free. A show you watched once.

[deadpan] **Your free trial of owning things just expired.**

You don't buy things anymore. [slower] You rent your whole life. One payment at a time.
```

- The `Your free trial of owning things just expired` pop-up keeps the running gag; background screens tease the device motif.

## Section 2: Reframe: You Stopped Buying, You Started Renting

Estimated time: `0:32-1:06`

Words: `120`

Purpose: Defuse the "subscriptions are just evil" reaction and set up the real question.

Visual goal: One device shown two ways. `OWN`: a phone in WIT's hand, normal, glowing softly. `RENT`: the same phone with a padlock slamming over the screen. "OWN" gets crossed out; "RENT" is stamped on.

Narration:

```text
Now — this is not a video about how subscriptions are evil and **you should throw your phone in a lake. Some are genuinely useful. Calm down.**

This is about something weirder.

We used to own things. You paid once, the thing was yours, the end. **A beautiful, boring transaction.**

A subscription is different. You don't buy the thing. You rent access to it. Pay every month, the screen turns on. Miss a payment, and your own device does this — [beat] — a little padlock appears, and **it looks at you like a disappointed parent.**

So here's the real question. How did almost everything — your apps, your shows, your software, even buttons inside your car — quietly turn into stuff you rent instead of stuff you own?
```

Approval check:

- The "some subscriptions are useful, calm down" line keeps the video from being anti-subscription.
- The padlock slamming over a screen is the clearest single image of "rent, not own."
- Ends on a clear question the rest of the video answers.

Voice revision notes:

- `[beat]` before "a little padlock appears" so the visual lands first.

## Section 3: The Spread: From Apps To Your Car

Words: `190`

Purpose: Show the model spreading — software, then entertainment, then physical hardware — so it feels everywhere.

Visual goal: Three beats on screens. A laptop with a program greying out behind a padlock. A monitor/TV showing a wall of generic streaming logos, each a glowing row that all vanish at once. A car dashboard screen with a heated-seat button behind a padlock. Quick `POV: you own nothing` card.

Narration:

```text
It started quietly, with software.

The program you used to buy once, in a box, became a monthly plan. Same program. Now it just... [beat] **holds itself hostage. Stop paying, and it greys out, like, "pay me, or the document gets it."**

Then your screens joined the party. Music, movies, shows — you don't own a single song or film. You rent a giant library, and the second you stop paying, the whole thing vanishes. **Poof. POV: you own nothing.**

And it's never one subscription. It's five. One for shows, one for movies, one for the other shows, one for music, and one for the app that organizes your other apps. Each one is small. Together? Bigger than the old cable bill everyone was running from. **We escaped one dungeon and built five smaller ones.**

And it didn't stop at screens. Some carmakers have tried putting heated seats — seats already sitting in the car you bought — behind a monthly fee.

[deadpan] **Your free trial of a warm bottom has expired.**
```

Approval check:

- The "five subs cost more than cable" beat reads as a fair comparison, not a stat.

## Section 4: Why Companies Love It: One Sale Becomes Forever

Estimated time: `2:04-2:59`

Words: `185`

Purpose: Reason 1 — recurring revenue is worth far more than a one-time sale, so companies redesigned around it.

Visual goal: A laptop turns into a little money machine: one coin drops once (a single sale) versus an endless stream of coins flowing from WIT's bank app to a company, month after month. WIT looks small beside the machine.

Narration:

```text
So why did everyone switch to this? Simple. **Follow the money. It's always the money.**

Imagine you sell a coffee machine. You make one sale, you say thank you, and then you wait — maybe years — hoping that person ever comes back.

Now imagine instead they pay you a little every month. Automatically. Possibly forever. Even on the months they don't touch the thing.

Which business would you rather own? [beat] **Yeah. Me too.**

A customer who buys once is worth a little. A customer who pays every month is worth a lot — because that payment just keeps coming. **Like rain. Or relatives.**

This is the magic word behind everything now: recurring. Recurring money is predictable. **A company can plan its entire future around your forgetfulness.**

So the goal quietly changed. It used to be: make something good enough that you buy it. Now it can also be: make something sticky enough that you never quite leave. **Less "great product." More "beautiful trap."**
```

Approval check:

- The coffee-machine comparison makes "recurring beats one-time" obvious without numbers.
- "Recurring" is introduced as the key word and shown on screen.
- Sets up reasons 2 and 3 (forgetting + hard to leave).

Voice revision notes:

- `[beat]` after "Which business would you rather own?"
- Keep "Like rain. Or relatives." light and quick.

## Section 5: The Free Trial Is A Countdown

Estimated time: `2:59-3:58`

Words: `195`

Purpose: Reason 2 — free trials, auto-renew, and ordinary forgetting keep people paying for things they don't use.

Visual goal: A bright `FREE TRIAL` button on a phone. A hidden countdown ticking behind it to `00:00`, then the button flips to `$2.99/mo`. The bank app shows a small mystery charge circled in red, repeating down the screen.

Narration:

```text
Here's the genius part. To get you in, they make it feel free.

"Start your free trial!" No charge today! Just pop in your card — you know, for no reason at all. [deadpan] **Totally normal. Strangers love holding your card.**

And the trial is real. It really is free. For seven days. After that, a tiny countdown you can't see hits zero, and "free" quietly becomes a payment.

And most people don't cancel on day seven. They forget. Not because they're careless — because forgetting is the design. The charge is small, the date is fuzzy, life is loud.

So the little payment just... continues. Forever. A ghost, living in your bank account. [beat] **Rent free. Well — not rent free. That's the whole problem.**

Open your bank statement right now and you'll probably find one. A mystery charge. Three dollars. Every month. For a thing you opened once, in a year you cannot name.

[deadpan] **Your free trial of financial awareness has expired.**
```

Approval check:

- The `FREE` button flipping to `$/mo` over a hidden countdown is the section's central visual joke.
- "Forgetting is the design, not your fault" protects the viewer from blame.
- "A ghost on your statement" sets up the payoff's "cancel the ghosts"; "rent free" pun ties to the OWN/RENT motif.

Voice revision notes:

- `[deadpan]` on "Strangers love holding your card." and on the closing gag.
- `[beat]` before "Rent free."

## Section 6: Easy In, No Way Out

Estimated time: `3:58-4:54`

Words: `185`

Purpose: Reason 3 — cancelling is made deliberately harder than joining (negative option billing).

Visual goal: A big, glowing, happy `SUBSCRIBE` button vs a tiny hidden `cancel` link. A phone-menu maze: account → settings → manage → "are you sure?" → "here's a discount" → "we'll miss you" → a phone number. WIT wandering the menus like a video-game level; a small `-1000 aura` flashes.

Narration:

```text
Okay, you say. I'll just cancel. [beat] **Oh, sweet child.**

Here's the strange law of subscriptions: signing up takes ten seconds. **Cancelling takes a vision quest.**

Getting in is a big, happy, glowing button. Getting out is **hidden somewhere in your phone like a final boss.**

You tap "account." Then "settings." Then "manage." Then "are you sure?" Then "here, have a discount." Then "we'll miss you." Then **a phone number that only answers between two and two-fifteen on a Tuesday.**

This even has a name. It's been called negative option billing — a fancy term for one simple trick: you keep paying unless you actively say stop.

So all the remembering, deciding, and escaping? Pushed onto you. **Minus a thousand aura points, and you didn't even do anything wrong.**

So a lot of people just... give up. They keep paying. Not because they want it — because **cancelling feels like a part-time job. With no salary. Which, fittingly, is also not a subscription.**
```

Approval check:

- The glowing `SUBSCRIBE` vs hidden `cancel` reads as unfair at a glance.
- "Negative option billing" is named and immediately explained in plain English.
- The "part-time job with no salary" line lands and callbacks the salary motif.

Voice revision notes:

- `[beat]` after "I'll just cancel."
- Keep "Minus a thousand aura points" light and quick; let the on-screen `-1000 aura` carry the joke for learners.

## Section 7: Payoff: The Product Is You Not Cancelling

Estimated time: `4:54-6:05`

Words: `190`

Purpose: Land the non-obvious insight calmly, tie the motif together, and end on a useful, repeatable action.

Visual goal: All of WIT's screens are locked behind padlocks. The money stream slows and stops. A clean board: the thing fades, `YOU — STAYING` remains. WIT turns the phone around and opens the bank app himself. Final image: the bank app showing his paycheck — the one screen not asking for money.

Narration:

```text
So why is everything a subscription now?

Because companies found something quietly genius. The most valuable thing they can sell isn't the app, or the show, or the heated seat. It's your forgetting. The month you meant to cancel, and didn't.

Once you see it, you can't unsee it. The product was never the thing. [slower] The product is you — staying.

Now, again: subscriptions aren't evil. Some are worth every cent. The point is just to notice. To know that "small and monthly" is a design, not an accident.

So every so often, pick up the phone that's been billing you — and turn it against them. Open the bank statement. Read the little charges out loud. Ask which ones you'd actually choose again today. Keep the ones you love. **Cancel the ghosts.**

Because in a world where almost everything is rented, the most powerful move you've got is remembering what you're paying for.

[beat] And then quietly enjoying the one thing in your life that is still not a monthly subscription. [deadpan] **Your salary. For now.**
```

Approval check:

- The insight ("the product is you not cancelling / your forgetting") is stated clearly and is non-obvious.
- One concrete, friendly action (read the statement, keep what you love, cancel ghosts) — no scolding.
- Ends on WIT turning the phone back against the system + the paycheck screen, with the dry "For now" button.

Voice revision notes:

- `[slower]` on "The product is you — staying."
- `[beat]` before the final line; `[deadpan]` on "Your salary. For now."

## Claim Safety Notes

### Safe Claims

- A subscription means paying a recurring price at intervals for access, not ownership; stop paying and access ends. (Wikipedia, subscription business model.)
- People often keep paying for subscriptions they no longer value because they don't realize they're still subscribed. (Wikipedia — supports the "forgetting is the design" framing.)
- Free trials exist to increase the chance a customer converts to paying. (Wikipedia — supports "the trial is a countdown.")
- "Negative option" billing — you keep paying unless you actively cancel — is a long-documented practice that has drawn consumer complaints. (Wikipedia, negative option billing.)
- The subscription model has spread from digital products into hardware/physical features. (Inference, examples kept generic.)

### Inferences Used Carefully

- "Recurring money is worth far more than a one-time sale, so companies redesigned around it." Framed as plain business logic, not a cited statistic.
- "Five streaming subscriptions often cost more than the old cable bill." Stated softly ("often" / "bigger than"), not as a measured figure.
- "Cancelling is deliberately harder than signing up." Supported by the negative-option concept and common experience; framed with examples, not as proof of intent for any named company.

### Claims Avoided

- No exact statistic on average number of subscriptions or average monthly spend (no live-confirmed source; in research Open Questions).
- No statement of the FTC "click-to-cancel" rule's current legal status (time-sensitive, unverified this session) — uses "negative option billing" as the documented anchor instead.
- No specific company named with an exact price or year (design-software year, car-feature price) — examples stay generic/soft.
- No claim that any specific company is acting illegally or "scamming."
- No "everyone is being ripped off / you are a fool" framing — the system explains it; the viewer is never blamed.

### Humor Safety

- Jokes and meme formats (`free trial of ___ has expired`, "POV", "aura points", "final boss", dad-joke puns) are comedic framing, not factual claims.
- Dark-tinted jokes ("throw your phone in a lake," "...your salary. For now.") are self-aimed/absurd and target the *system*, never a person, group, or protected category. Nothing encourages real harm.
- Trending phrases are always paired with an on-screen visual so meaning reads from context for English learners.
