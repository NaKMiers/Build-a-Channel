# Research brief - The Economics of Tipping

## Central question

Why does a voluntary payment at the end of a service transaction feel compulsory, and how does that feeling help finance a US service-labor system?

## Familiar moment

You order a coffee, sandwich, or sit-down meal. Before you can finish paying, a screen offers tip choices while the worker waits nearby. You know you can choose zero, but the decision can feel like a public test of whether you are fair.

## Common belief

A tip is simply a personal reward for especially good service. The amount is voluntary, so the customer has full control.

## Contradiction

Tipping is legally voluntary in the ordinary case, but it operates inside wage rules, platform defaults, and social norms that make the customer partly responsible for worker pay. In a nationally representative 2023 US survey, 72% of adults said tipping was expected in more places than five years earlier, while only 34% said it was very or extremely easy to know whether to tip. [C05, S04]

## One-sentence reframe

Tipping is not only a thank-you. It is a decentralized wage decision, made transaction by transaction, under social pressure and product design.

## Unit economics

Illustration, not a market average: on a $20 restaurant bill, a 15.4% tip is $3.08 and the total paid is $23.08. The arithmetic is $20 x 0.154 = $3.08. Square reported a 15.4% average tip on restaurant transactions in its US food-and-beverage seller data in 2024. This illustration shows the participant's extra payment, not a claim about any one restaurant or worker. [C07, S03]

At the federal floor, an employer that takes the FLSA tip credit may pay a tipped employee a direct cash wage of at least $2.13 per hour and claim up to $5.12 per hour of tip credit toward the $7.25 federal minimum wage. The employer must make up any weekly shortfall. State law can require a higher direct wage or prohibit the credit. [C01, C02, S01]

## Behavioral engine

The key mechanism is norm compliance under ambiguity. People use shared rules when they do not know what amount is expected, especially when declining a payment is visible or feels interpersonal. Pew found that 49% of US adults said whether tipping is a choice or obligation depends on the situation, and 77% said service quality is a major factor in their decision. [C06, S04]

Screen design can add an anchor or default to that ambiguous decision. A 2020 Management Science field experiment randomly assigned tip recommendations across 94,571 orders from 24,637 customers of an app-based laundry pickup, cleaning, and delivery service. Recommendations changed tip amounts, but the study did not find effects on satisfaction, return frequency, or bill size. This supports a narrow claim about recommendation design in that setting, not a universal claim that all tip screens raise tips. [C08, S05]

A separate simulated limited-service coffee-shop study found more negative feelings toward screen-based payment methods than tip jars, without higher tip sizes in that scenario. Its authors explicitly note the hypothetical setting as a limitation. [C09, S06]

## Hidden system and mid-video reveal

The mid-video reveal is that a tip and a mandatory service charge are not merely two names for the same extra amount. The IRS says a payment is a tip when the customer voluntarily decides to pay it and determines the amount. A compulsory service charge is employer income and, when distributed, generally wages paid by the employer. A digital prompt that requires a customer to select more than zero is listed by the IRS as a service-charge example. [C03, C04, S02]

That distinction changes the story. A voluntary tip can feel like personal gratitude while also helping determine a worker's take-home pay. A compulsory charge is a price decision by the business and has different payroll and tax treatment.

## Case study

Use the federal tip-credit system as the case study, with an explicit US limitation. The federal rule does not mean every tipped worker is paid $2.13 per hour: employers must make up shortfalls, and state laws can be more protective. It does show why the customer-facing tip is connected to the employer's wage obligation in some jurisdictions. [C01, C02, S01]

Square's 2024 US seller data provides scale without pretending it represents every restaurant: it reported that the average restaurant worker in its data earned nearly 23% of income in tips and that the average restaurant transaction tip was 15.4%. [C10, C07, S03]

## Counterargument and boundary conditions

Tipping can reward attentive service and can provide meaningful income to workers. It is not accurate to say every tip replaces wages or that every digital prompt is coercive. Federal law requires employers using the tip credit to make up the difference to the applicable federal minimum wage, and states may set stronger rules. [C02, S01]

The norms also vary sharply by setting. In Pew's 2023 survey, 92% of adults who eat at sit-down restaurants said they always or often tip there, compared with 25% buying coffee and 12% eating at a fast-casual restaurant. These are self-reported US survey results, not rules for what anyone should do. [C11, S04]

## Human price

The visible cost is the added payment. The less visible cost is decision fatigue and moral uncertainty every time the checkout moment asks the customer to translate an unclear norm into money. For workers, income can be variable and partly tied to customer traffic, service context, and norms. For employers, tips can support compensation structures that would otherwise need a different posted-price and wage model. This final sentence is an inference from the wage rules, survey evidence, and Square data. [C01, C05, C10]
