## Derivation notes

**@YOU.** The bookend is the end of an ordinary day being counted. The script opens on "Count the
times you laughed today. Now count the things that were actually funny" and closes on the same two
lines, reframed. The day being counted is a workday with a meeting in it ("your manager's weak line")
that ends with friends and a phone. Costume taken from that day per the bookend rule: a short-sleeve
polo shirt, untucked. The polo is new on the channel: its flat two-point knit collar, short
two-button placket and ribbed sleeve bands give V2 its thin internal detail lines without a second
filled body shape, and nothing Toss has worn before has that short placket. The channel has used the
hoodie, a camp-collar shirt, crew-neck sweatshirts, a jacket over a tee, a track jacket, a t-shirt, a
work shirt, a pyjama top, a v-neck jumper over a collar, a collared shirt with an apron, and a
quarter-zip. Colour is lavender `#B79AD9`, assigned last. The rejections, so a later reader does not
"fix" it: coral, dusty teal and olive are the chapter colours; orange collides with the coral modern
ground, the collision projects 3, 12 and 13 all hit; grass green belongs to the friend and they share
the shift and the tease; sky blue belongs to the manager and they share the audit; tan belongs to the
student; brown belongs to the chimp; red is the episode's negation signal; golden yellow was Toss's
colour two videos ago and sits lowest in contrast on a cream card; cobalt and Toss blue are reserved
and read as the old hoodie. Lavender was last worn by Toss in project 14, four videos ago, in a
different silhouette. His prop is the phone, derived and not inherited: the script hands it to him
in the texting mismatch ("Ha ha. Lol. A face crying with laughter. Hundreds of laughs a week") and in
the shift ("or at least the same phone call"), and gives him no other object.

**@STUDENT.** The psychology pillar is Robert Provine's sidewalk study and it is on screen in four
consecutive beats plus two callbacks in the mismatch: "He and his students walked through shopping
malls, sidewalks and campus hallways, and every time someone nearby laughed, they wrote down what had
been said just before it", the sorting, the speaker laughing more than the listener, the punctuation
effect, "remember Provine's shopping malls", and "Provine's thirty to one". **Provine himself is NOT
cast, and the reason is the likeness rule.** A real named person on screen must be drawn from his
documented appearance, and no reliable portrait of Provine could be verified for this run: his
Wikipedia entry carries no image and the UMBC biography and memorial pages carry no usable
photograph. Inventing a beard or glasses for him would be fabricating a likeness. So Provine stays
narration, exactly like a named researcher who is never depicted, and the on-screen work of the
study is carried by one of the students the script itself puts in the malls. **The scenes stage must
not draw Provine as a person.** His study is shown through this student, the overheard pairs as
generic figures, and diagrams. If a verified portrait is found later, a Provine sheet can replace
this entry; it is not a gap to fill by guessing. The student is a woman, derived from nothing in the
script, which genders none of them. Nineteen nineties campus wear: a tan corduroy jacket over a white
tee, shoulder-length straight hair held back by a plain headband. Her prop is the script's own
action: a small spiral notebook and a pencil, plus a short stack of blank index cards for the
sorting. The notebook is the sheet's text trap and it is enumerated in her NO TEXT block.

**@CHIMP.** The whole first half of the anthropology runs on apes, and the script never puts a
human ancestor on screen there, so there is no ancestral human in this cast. There is no default
ancestor. Moments: "Watch two young chimpanzees wrestle", the play face that van Hooff named, the
tickled apes of Davila-Ross, "For animals with real teeth that mattered", the grooming that Dunbar
describes, and then the mismatch's "Your laugh is still the play face of an ape". That is the widest
spread of any non-Toss figure in the script. One sheet, one young chimpanzee. **The wrestle needs two
of them, and they are two copies of this one design**, told apart by pose and position only; the
scenes stage must not invent a second ape sheet. The other species Davila-Ross tickled (orangutans,
gorillas, bonobos) and the human babies appear once each and are generic scene figures, not cast.
Construction keeps the channel's grammar and adds only what makes an ape read at doodle scale: a
brown head with a large tan face disc, two round ears, a brown fur body standing in for the garment,
and long arms. **The play face is drawn with NO TEETH at all.** The script's point is that the play
face is the opposite of a threat, and bared teeth are a threat display in every primate, so the open
mouth is a relaxed empty oval and the NEGATIVE kills teeth, fangs and snarls by name.

**@FRIEND.** Four moments with the widest spread among the modern figures: "a friend's story that
went nowhere", "You were telling your friend you are glad to be there ... Your friend heard it", the
people you care about trading typed laughs, the shift's "the people you would most want beside you"
and "the same room, or at least the same phone call", and the tease's hardest laugh of the year,
"who was there". The friend is one half of the shift's contrast, the real laugh, and the manager is
the other half, the polite one. Project 10's rule applies: when the script's claim is A versus B,
both are cast. Grass green roll-neck jumper and a round cloud of tight curly hair, so the silhouette
reads from across a room. The script gives the friend no object of their own, so the prop block is
the oversized hands and head close-up the cast skill specifies, with the phone appearing only in the
call pose and drawn identical to Toss's.

**@BOSS.** Three moments, all in the most pointed passage of the mismatch: the hook's "laughing on cue
so the room stays comfortable", "when you catch yourself laughing at your manager's weak line", "You
were telling your manager you are not a threat ... Your manager heard it", and the shift's "The
polite laugh at work is not a lie". He is the polite-laugh pole of the contrast. **He must not look
like an antagonist.** The video's argument is that the polite laugh is goodwill toward someone you
need to get along with, so a menacing manager would contradict the script. His NEGATIVE kills the
scowl, the pointing finger and the crossed arms by name, on the project 15 precedent for a character
who exists to disprove the viewer's fear. He also gets Provine's finding as a pose: the speaker
laughing at his own ordinary line, more than anyone listening. Sky blue shirt with a small red tie,
neat side-parted hair, rectangular glasses. No prop is given by the script, so the prop block is
hands and head.

**Separation between the three modern adults.** They share an era and would drift into each other,
so each sheet names the other two's markers in its NEGATIVE. Toss: two-peak tuft, lavender polo with
a short placket, no collar points standing up. The friend: round cloud of curls, grass green
roll-neck, no collar, no glasses. The manager: neat side parting, rectangular glasses, sky blue
buttoned shirt with a pointed collar and a red tie. The student is a nineteen nineties figure with
a headband and a tan jacket and never shares a frame with the present-day three.

**The recurring red X.** Checked against the cast before use, per the standing rule from projects 14
and 15. Nobody wears red except the manager's small tie. The red X must never land on the manager,
because negating him contradicts the shift, which tells the viewer to let the polite laugh go
without the audit. It may land on the audit itself, the "fake" verdict, and on a typed laugh.

**Refused a slot.** Provine, for the likeness reason above. Sophie Scott, Robin Dunbar, Alfred
Radcliffe-Brown and Greg Bryant are named and never described doing anything on screen; their
studies are drawn through generic subjects (the comedy watchers with the frozen sleeve, the in-laws
teasing, the listeners in twenty four societies). The laugh itself was the strongest abstract
candidate, since the script calls it "a message" and "the joke is just the envelope it arrived in",
and it was refused: a laugh is a sound with nothing to turn around, and every way of drawing it on a
sheet invites lettered "ha ha", which the no-text rule forbids. The scenes stage carries it as a
continuity ledger motif instead: three short curved charcoal `#2F3133` sound strokes fanning out from
the mouth, never letters. The band of thirty or forty and the in-law pair each appear in one passage
and are generic. The phone is Toss's prop.
