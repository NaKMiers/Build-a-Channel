## Raw Idea

Cheap products keep getting worse.

## Clean Angle

Cheap products look affordable, but many are really products with the future removed.

This video is about how a cheap product looks like a good deal, but is actually a product where durability, repairability, materials, or long-term usefulness have been quietly taken out.

## Viewer Curiosity

What will make the viewer think: `Wait, that's true. Why does that happen?`

- The viewer has bought something cheap that broke, stretched, stopped working, or became annoying faster than expected.
- The contradiction is easy to feel: the price looks lower now, but the product may cost more later.
- The visual question is clear: why does the product look normal, but act hollow, thinner, weaker, or disposable?
- The topic creates a broad modern-life suspicion without blaming the viewer for buying affordable things.

## Channel Fit

- Lane: `money, business, modern life, consumer behavior`
- Tone fit: `smart, simple, funny, dry`
- English learner fit: strong, because the topic naturally teaches everyday words such as `cheap`, `affordable`, `quality`, `break`, `repair`, `replace`, `warranty`, `durable`, and `disposable`
- Why now: many viewers feel that normal products, clothes, devices, furniture, and household items do not last the way they expect, while prices still feel high

## Angle Package

- Topic: cheap products and declining quality
- Sharp angle: a cheap product is not always a bargain; sometimes it is a normal product with the future quietly removed
- Contradiction: cheap looks like saving money, but can become paying later through replacement, frustration, or lost usefulness
- Recurring visual metaphor: `future removed from the product`
- Viewer pain: the viewer buys something normal, then it breaks, shrinks, wears out, cannot be repaired, or feels worse than expected
- WIT role: WIT is excited by a cheap price tag, then slowly defeated as pieces of the product fall off, repair doors disappear, and the product turns into a receipt printer
- Real-life objects: cheap shirt, shoe, phone charger, chair, headphones, plastic box, zipper, price tag, warranty card, repair manual, trash bin, replacement receipt
- Thumbnail tension: WIT holds a product with a big `CHEAP?` label while the product is missing screws, fabric, warranty, or a tiny piece labeled `future`
- First 10 seconds: WIT buys a cheap object with a happy price tag. By second `5`, a hidden label appears: `future not included`. By second `8`, the object breaks while the price tag smiles. By second `10`, the narrator asks why cheap products keep feeling worse.
- English learner value: the video uses simple object-based English, repeated useful phrases, and concrete examples instead of abstract economics
- Final insight: the cheapest product is not always the lowest price; sometimes the real cost is hidden in how soon you have to replace it

## Main Risk

The topic can become too broad or too documentary-like.

Do not make the video a general essay about capitalism, manufacturing, or inflation. Keep the channel version centered on one repeatable visual joke:

`The product is cheap because pieces of its future were removed before WIT bought it.`

## Required Fixes Before Research

1. Pick `3-5` concrete product categories only, such as clothes, chargers, chairs, headphones, and household tools.
2. Avoid real brand accusations unless research strongly supports them and usage is safe.
3. Explain the system without blaming people for buying affordable products.
4. Make `cheap` different from `bad`. The point is not that all cheap things are bad; the point is that some products hide the real cost in replacement.
5. Build a reference board with product photos, safe mockups, broken-object visual jokes, and simple before/after comparisons before scripting.
