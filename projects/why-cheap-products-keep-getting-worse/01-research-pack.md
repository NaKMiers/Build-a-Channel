## Topic Intake Snapshot

- Working title: `Why Cheap Products Keep Getting Worse`
- Sharp angle: Cheap products look affordable, but many are really products with the future removed.
- Main contradiction: Cheap looks like saving money now, but can become paying later through replacement, repair problems, frustration, or lost usefulness.
- Recurring motif: `future not included`
- WIT role: WIT is excited by a low price tag, then discovers missing screws, weaker fabric, blocked repair doors, short warranties, and receipts that keep printing.
- Final insight: The cheapest product is not always the lowest-cost product. Sometimes the real cost is hidden in how soon you have to replace it.
- Main risk: The topic can become too broad, too anti-company, or too documentary-like. Keep it object-based and avoid claiming all cheap products are bad.

## Working Thesis

Cheap products do not always get worse because one company secretly decides to make bad things. The stronger explanation is that the visible price is under pressure while the invisible future of the product gets cut: better materials, repairable parts, simpler designs, longer testing, spare parts, and long-term support. The product still looks like a normal shirt, chair, phone, charger, or appliance, but some of its future usefulness has been quietly removed before the buyer ever sees it.

## Research Questions

3. What hidden parts of a product's future can be removed: material, repairability, parts, software updates, warranty, or testing time?
4. Why do companies and consumers both get pulled toward cheaper, faster, more replaceable products?
5. What can be safely shown on screen as mockups or generic objects?
6. Which claims are strong enough for a funny explainer, and which need more verification before scripting?

## What People Think

Fact:
Viewers already recognize the symptom. The Vox video had about `4.79M` visible views, More Perfect Union's clothing video had about `6.06M`, and the furniture video had about `1.07M`. This suggests the feeling "my stuff is worse now" is already a strong viewer pain.

Inference:
People usually experience this as a personal betrayal: "I bought a normal thing, but it acted like a temporary thing." That is better for this channel than a broad lecture about capitalism.

Example:
WIT buys a cheap charger. It works, smiles for three seconds, then the cable neck starts bending like it has already given up on life.

Open question:
The script should decide whether to mention exact YouTube view counts or simply use the reference videos internally for demand and packaging direction.

## What Is Actually Happening

Fact:
Product quality can decline through less obvious choices, not only through visible "cheapness." Vox and Wirecutter both point to materials, production speed, repair difficulty, added features, and price pressure as recurring mechanisms.

Fact:
The European Parliament frames repair as a policy issue because many goods that could be repaired are discarded, and because cost or lack of repair services can push consumers toward replacement.

Fact:
The European Commission smartphone and tablet rules make repairability, battery cycles, drop resistance, spare parts, and software access visible regulatory concerns. This is strong evidence that "future usefulness" can be designed into or out of a product.

Fact:
Ellen MacArthur Foundation describes fashion as increasingly disposable and fast-moving, with production pace and fast fashion changing how clothing is designed, sold, and used.

Inference:
The hidden system is not "cheap equals bad." The hidden system is "a low visible price can be maintained by removing invisible future value."

Example:
A cheap shirt may still look good on the rack because the missing future is not visible yet. The missing future appears after washing, stretching, fading, weak seams, or pilling.

## Why It Keeps Happening

Fact:
Wirecutter reports that added appliance features increase complexity, while low market prices make some repairs feel uneconomical compared with replacement.

Fact:
The European Commission's phone and tablet page treats longer lifetime, repairability, battery life, spare parts, and OS updates as levers that can reduce replacement and acquisition costs.

Fact:
iFixit and Repair.org both describe repair restrictions around parts, tools, documentation, diagnostics, software locks, and firmware access.

Inference:
Consumers want lower prices, faster shipping, more features, and newer styles. Companies compete on those visible things. The hidden costs show up later as weak parts, short support, complicated repairs, or products that are easier to replace than fix.

Inference:
Repair becomes less attractive when the repair is slow, expensive, inconvenient, or blocked. Replacement becomes the default even when the product is not spiritually dead yet.

Example:
WIT opens a tiny door labeled `REPAIR`. Behind it is another door labeled `AUTHORIZED REPAIR`. Behind that is a receipt for almost the price of a new product.

## English Learner Support

- Useful words: `cheap`, `affordable`, `durable`, `break`, `repair`, `replace`, `warranty`, `quality`, `materials`, `parts`, `support`, `disposable`
- Useful phrases: `looks like a bargain`, `costs more later`, `built to last`, `easy to repair`, `not worth fixing`, `hidden cost`
- Jargon to explain simply: `planned obsolescence` means a product becomes old, broken, unsupported, or unwanted sooner than the buyer expects; `repairability` means how easy it is to fix; `circular economy` means keeping products and materials useful for longer.
- Phrases to avoid: dense economics terms, moral judgment about poor buyers, brand-specific accusations, and native-only idioms that hide the point.

## Safe Claims

- Many viewers feel that ordinary products do not last as well as they expect.
- Product quality can be affected by material choices, production speed, design complexity, repair access, and price competition.
- Repairability is a real policy and consumer issue, especially for electronics and appliances.
- Some modern products are harder or less economical to repair because parts, tools, diagnostics, software, or repair information may be limited.
- Fast fashion and fast replacement cycles can encourage products that are used for shorter periods.
- Cheap does not always mean bad, and expensive does not always mean durable.
- The best script angle is hidden future cost, not "all companies are evil."

## Claims To Avoid

- Avoid saying all cheap products are bad.
- Avoid saying every company intentionally designs products to break unless a specific source supports that claim.
- Avoid claiming one named brand is guilty of a specific bad practice unless separately verified.
- Avoid saying old products were always better. Some older products were simpler, but nostalgia can exaggerate.
- Avoid saying repair is always the better choice. Repair can be expensive, slow, unsafe, or impractical.
- Avoid using exact statistics from blocked sources that were not inspected in this run.
- Avoid making this a pure sustainability documentary. The viewer pain is buying normal things that lose future value.

## Open Questions

- Pick `3-5` product categories for the script. Recommended: shirt, phone/tablet, appliance, chair/furniture, charger/headphones.
- Decide whether to use the term `planned obsolescence` in the script or keep the simpler phrase `future not included`.
- If the script needs official U.S. repair-policy evidence, re-access the FTC report through a non-blocked route or use another accessible official source.
- If using exact textile or e-waste statistics, verify them again from accessible primary sources before narration.
- Decide whether the video should mention brands at all. The safer version uses generic products and fake mockups.
