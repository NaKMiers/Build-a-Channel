## Raw Idea

Young people and career-switchers can send hundreds of applications and still fail to land a first or entry-level job. It feels personal, but the bottom of the career ladder has changed.

## Clean Angle

You need experience to get a job, and a job to get experience. The first rung that once solved this problem is now missing. Four overlapping forces make the first step harder: ghost jobs, AI changing beginner tasks, higher experience requirements, and a low-hire job market.

## Viewer Curiosity

`Wait, an entry-level job wants experience before it will give me experience. How am I supposed to start?`

## Angle Package

- **Topic:** the disappearing first step into work.
- **Contradiction:** `entry-level` jobs increasingly expect applicants to arrive with experience, while employers say they cannot find qualified workers.
- **Recurring visual metaphor:** a career ladder with its first rung missing. Each reason removes or raises another piece of the starting ladder.
- **Secondary motif:** job listings that fade into ghosts and resumes falling into an empty inbox.
- **Viewer pain:** repeated applications, silence, rejection, and the feeling that something is personally wrong with them.
- **WIT role:** eager beginner -> confused applicant -> suspicious ghost hunter -> exhausted ladder climber -> calm, practical reframe.
- **Real-life objects:** resume stack, laptop, job-board listing, auto-reply email, rejection email, graduation cap, office door, ladder, and missing rung.
- **Final insight:** you are not simply failing to climb. The first step became smaller, higher, or fake. Understanding which barrier you are facing helps you target real openings and show proof of ability.

## The 4 Reasons

1. **Some advertised jobs are ghosts.** A listing may stay public without an immediate intention to hire, which makes visible demand look larger than real hiring.
2. **AI is changing some beginner work.** In highly exposed roles, automation can absorb or reshape the simple tasks that once trained junior workers. Evidence is still early, so AI must not be presented as the only cause.
3. **The entry bar moved upward.** Employers increasingly value demonstrated experience, and the measurable shift toward more experienced candidates is especially strong in technology roles.
4. **The market is stuck in a low-hire phase.** Even when employment looks stable, fewer people move into new jobs. First-time applicants have less experience to use as a risk-reduction signal, so they feel the freeze first.

Net mechanism: employers reduce risk by asking applicants to arrive already trained, while applicants need a real first job to become trained.

## The Real Mechanism

1. Employers post an apparent opening.
2. The applicant sees a possible first rung.
3. The rung is fake, automated, raised by experience requirements, or missing because hiring is weak.
4. The applicant receives silence or rejection and blames themselves.
5. Employers continue using experience as a low-risk filter, which preserves the same trap.
6. The practical turn is to identify real hiring signals and show direct proof of useful work, without pretending that better resume tricks can fix the whole market.

## Must Stay Honest

- AI is one reason, not the whole explanation. Current evidence is strongest in highly exposed occupations and does not prove an economy-wide AI job collapse.
- Do not claim every entry-level job now demands experience. The measurable tightening is strongest in tech, while the wider evidence shows employers placing more value on demonstrated experience.
- Ghost jobs are real enough to attract official research and proposed regulation, but estimates vary by definition and method. Do not put one universal ghost-job percentage on screen without research-pack verification.
- A low-hire market is not the same as mass unemployment. The important distinction is fewer new matches and less movement into jobs.
- Do not promise that one tactic guarantees a job. Practical advice must be framed as ways to find stronger signals and improve odds.
- Blame incentives and structure, not applicants, age groups, recruiters as individuals, or AI as a cartoon supervillain.

## Main Risk

Bleakness and overclaiming. A video about an impossible first job can become rage bait, generic career advice, or AI panic. Keep the humor aimed at the system, show the four causes separately, scope evidence by country and industry, and finish with an honest practical section about spotting real openings and showing work evidence.
