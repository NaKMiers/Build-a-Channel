## Draft Strategy

- Core thesis: A first job can be harder to get when four systems overlap. Across several of them, employers may prefer applicants who bring less uncertainty and need less training.
- Recurring motif: The career ladder is the thesis object. Each step is called a `rung`. The first rung turns transparent, loses its practice tasks, moves higher, or sits behind a frozen door. The video closes by checking whether that rung is real before climbing harder.
- Secondary motif: Experience becomes a `risk receipt` showing that another organization has already seen the applicant work.
- WIT arc: S1 eager -> fooled; S2 suspicious; S3 alarmed; S4 frustrated; S5 exhausted; S6 recognizes the loop; S7 calm investigator; S8 grounded climber; S9 warm accomplice.
- Script risk: The topic can become AI panic, recruiter blame, or fake motivational advice. Mitigation: scope every statistic, make structural incentives the target, keep practical signals explicitly uncertain, and never promise a job.
- Retention chain: Sections 1-8 rotate distinct handoff devices: count promise -> image transformation -> paradox -> evidence tease -> pattern reveal -> action command -> withheld verdict -> person-specific handoff. Each next section pays off the handoff immediately. Section 9 closes with no new open loop.

## Humor System

- Running gag: The ladder keeps receiving corporate improvements that make it less useful: an invisible rung, no practice rung, a rung near the ceiling, and a revolving door on energy-saving mode.
- Register: Dry, cheeky, slightly rude, and aimed at the system. The applicant is never the joke target.
- Vetted line bank:
  - `Entry-level. Previous entry required.`
  - `The OPEN sign is real. The door is painted on the wall.`

- Real-name rule: Neutral factual mentions of publishers or researchers are allowed only when needed for sourcing. Real people, generations, recruiters, companies, and immigrant groups are never joke targets.
- Humor safety: No slurs, no protected-group jokes, no attack on unemployed people, and no claim that every employer is dishonest.

## Section 2: Reason 1: The Rung Is A Ghost

Estimated time: `0:16-1:32`

Words: `228`

Purpose: Explain why an advertised opening may not represent an immediate hire from outside the company, without claiming that every unfilled listing is fake.

Visual goal: Suspicious WIT tests a first ladder rung made from a job card. It turns transparent. Behind it sit four folders: `FUTURE POOL`, `INTERNAL`, `ON HOLD`, and `OUTDATED`. No statistic appears in this section.

Narration:

```text
Reason one: the job is visible, but it may not be truly open right now.

Think of a career as a ladder. Each step is called a rung. Your first job should be the first rung.

One common use of ghost job means a public job ad when the company is not ready to hire someone from outside right now.

Sometimes a company keeps a listing open to collect resumes for future roles. Sometimes it already has an internal candidate. Sometimes the role is paused or canceled. And sometimes the listing is simply old.

That does not mean every job ad with no reply is a secret corporate crime. A real role can take a long time to fill. A copied listing can stay online after the original changes. A recruiter may also be handling a large number of applications.

But from the applicant's side, many of these explanations can look frustratingly similar.

The APPLY button works. But no real hiring process starts.

It is an OPEN sign with a door painted on the wall. Very welcoming. Technically excellent paint.

So yes, some visible job ads give weak evidence that anyone is hiring now. You can spend an hour changing your resume for a job whose company forgot to hold a funeral.

Sometimes the job is real. The listing stays solid, but part of the practice rung disappears.
```

## Section 7: How To Test A Rung

Estimated time: `6:19-7:58`

Words: `306`

Purpose: Give the viewer useful agency by separating clues about a live role from proof of ability, while clearly stating that none of the clues guarantees a hire.

Visual goal: Calm investigator WIT tests three clues across three distinct base scenes: a careers page labeled `RECENT + SPECIFIC`, a hiring-process board labeled `REAL PEOPLE`, and a portfolio desk labeled `MATCHED PROOF`. A large label says `CLUES, NOT GUARANTEES`. WIT builds one portable rung from an existing work sample.

Narration:

```text
Start with three clues. They are not proof.

First, look for signs that the role is recent and specific.

Is it on the company's own careers page? Does it name a real team, project, location, or hiring need? A fresh, detailed listing is not proof. It is simply a stronger signal than a vague ad that has been copied for months.

That vague ad may be a rung. It may also be office wallpaper.

Second, look for signs that real people are hiring.

Does the listing name a recruiter or team member? Is the company describing real interview steps or a real timeline? Silence does not prove a job is fake. But signs of a real hiring process make the listing easier to trust.

If the only active participant is the APPLY button, lower your confidence, not your self-worth.

Third, show proof that matches the work.

If the job asks for analysis, show one clear analysis. If it asks for writing, show a short sample. A class project, volunteer project, or a project you made by yourself can show what you can do before a company gives you the perfect title.

Use work you already made, or make one general sample for your portfolio. Do not build a free custom project for every mysterious company on the internet. Your unemployment is not their unlimited free trial.

These are clues, not guarantees.

They will not repair a weak market. They will not make a frozen hiring budget move. And they cannot turn every rejection into useful feedback.

But they may help you spend less time on painted doors and show stronger evidence to the people behind real ones.

That changes the final verdict.

Once you can name the broken rung, the problem stops looking so personal.

And it is not the verdict you have been giving yourself.
```

Approval check:

- The company-site and human-activity signals do not label silence as fraud.
- Proof-of-work examples are concrete and do not recommend unpaid custom work for employers.
- `CLUES, NOT GUARANTEES` is held long enough to protect the practical advice.
- The withheld verdict is answered by the first line of Section 8.

## Claim Safety Notes

### Safe Claims

- Some public listings may represent future candidate pools, internal candidates, paused or canceled roles, duplicates, or outdated posts. The script avoids claiming a universal ghost-job rate.
- A Stanford payroll study found a `16% relative` employment decline for workers aged `22-25` in the most AI-exposed occupations after firm-level controls.
- A broad Yale analysis found no discernible economy-wide labor disruption attributable to AI in its broad measures.
- Among US tech postings that stated experience in the cited analysis, the share asking for at least `5 years` rose from `37%` in Q2 2022 to `42%` in Q2 2025. The narration explicitly says this is not an entry-level-only study.
- Relevant internships were leading tie-breakers between otherwise equal graduate candidates in the cited NACE employer survey.
- The US hires rate was `3.2%` in April 2026, near its weakest since 2013. The script identifies it as an aggregate US figure.

### Inferences Used Carefully

- AI can remove or reshape some practice tasks. The narration uses `can`, `may`, and `pieces of that work`, not an absolute prediction.
- Experience functions like a risk receipt. This is a metaphorical synthesis of the employer preference evidence.
- More applications can create more recruiter noise and less individual feedback. The script uses `can`, not a measured universal effect.
- Recent, specific listings and signs of a real hiring process are practical clues, not proof that a role is live.
- A work sample may provide evidence of ability, but it does not replace every experience requirement or guarantee a job.

### Claims Avoided

- No claim that AI caused a `16%` loss across all entry-level jobs.
- No claim that AI is the main cause of the first-job problem.
- No claim that most job listings are fake or malicious.
- No use of the invalid openings-versus-hires gap as a ghost-job count.
- No claim that every entry-level role asks for five years of experience.
- No claim that stable employment means easy entry into the market.
- No promise that internships, networking, work samples, or resume changes guarantee employment.
- No blame aimed at applicants, individual recruiters, generations, public figures, companies, or immigrant groups.
- The working title and hook keep the direct numbered packaging promise from topic intake; the body scopes each factor and does not present the four-part model as proven universal causation.

### Humor Safety

- `Forgot to hold a funeral` targets an outdated listing, not death, unemployed people, or a named organization.
- `Unlimited free trial` targets exploitative incentives and explicitly discourages unpaid custom work.
- `No meeting required` rejects conspiracy thinking and keeps the system, not a person, as the joke target.
- `Suspiciously powerful subscribe button` makes the lack of an employment promise explicit.
- No profanity is needed; the topic's own contradictions carry the cheeky tone.
