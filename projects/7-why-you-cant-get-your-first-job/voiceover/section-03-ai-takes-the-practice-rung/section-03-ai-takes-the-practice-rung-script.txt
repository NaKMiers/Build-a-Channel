Reason two: AI can take part of the training job.

Many beginners start with smaller tasks. Draft this. Sort that. Summarize this document. Find the first answer.

Those tasks create value, but they also create practice. They are how a beginner learns what good work looks like.

Now AI can do pieces of some of that work quickly. Great for the task. Awkward for the training. A company may need fewer people for the first draft. Or it may expect one junior worker to use AI, check the result, and make a harder decision much sooner.

In one large US payroll study, researchers looked at workers aged twenty-two to twenty-five.

The researchers accounted for changes inside each company.

In jobs where AI could do many of the tasks, the study found a sixteen-percent relative employment decline for that group.

Relative means compared with the study's comparison path. It does not mean sixteen percent of all entry-level jobs disappeared.

It is an early signal in selected occupations. It does not prove that AI caused the whole change. Another broad study found no clear economy-wide AI disruption in the measures it examined.

So the honest version is smaller, but still important.

AI does not always take the whole ladder. Sometimes it takes the homework and leaves you the exam.

AI can remove the practice. The next barrier keeps the work, then asks you to have practiced somewhere else.
