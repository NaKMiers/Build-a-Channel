Reason four: companies are hiring slowly, even when they are not firing many people.<break time="0.45s"/>
<break time="0.45s"/>
Economists call this a low-hire, low-fire market. Many people inside companies keep their jobs, while fewer new people come through the door.<break time="0.45s"/>
<break time="0.45s"/>
Look at April 2026.<break time="0.45s"/>
<break time="0.45s"/>
During that month, new hires were equal to three-point-two percent of total US employment.<break time="0.45s"/>
<break time="0.45s"/>
That was near the weakest hires rate in more than a decade.<break time="0.45s"/>
<break time="0.45s"/>
At the same time, separations were also low. So employment could still grow slowly. From far away, the building looked fine.<break time="0.45s"/>
<break time="0.45s"/>
From the entrance, the revolving door looked frozen.<break time="0.45s"/>
<break time="0.45s"/>
This matters because a first-time applicant is usually outside. They have no previous employer to lower the perceived risk. And they may be competing with people who already carry that experience receipt.<break time="0.45s"/>
<break time="0.45s"/>
The market is not necessarily firing everyone. It has simply put the revolving door on energy-saving mode.<break time="0.45s"/>
<break time="0.45s"/>
This is why stable employment and a terrible personal job search can exist at the same time. Aggregate employment can stay stable because few people leave. You are trying to become one of the fewer new hires.<break time="0.45s"/>
<break time="0.45s"/>
The official message is simple: ladder available. Door unavailable.<break time="0.45s"/>
<break time="0.45s"/>
Now put all four reasons together. Ghost listings, changed tasks, a higher bar, and a slow door do not look related at first.<break time="0.45s"/>
<break time="0.45s"/>
Four different barriers. One quiet pattern: the safer-looking hire keeps winning.<break time="0.45s"/>
