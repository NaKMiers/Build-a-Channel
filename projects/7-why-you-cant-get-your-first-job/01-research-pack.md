## Topic Intake Snapshot

- Working title: `4 Reasons You Can't Get Your First Job Anymore`
- Sharp angle: The first rung of the career ladder has become fake, automated, raised, or temporarily missing.
- Main contradiction: Employers want proof of experience from people who need a first job to gain experience.
- Recurring motif: A career ladder with the first rung missing. Each reason changes one part of the ladder.
- WIT role: Eager beginner -> confused applicant -> suspicious ghost hunter -> exhausted ladder climber -> calm practical observer.
- Final insight: The viewer is not simply bad at climbing. Different barriers require different responses, and no resume trick can repair the whole market.
- Main risk: Turning a multi-cause problem into AI panic, recruiter blame, or a promise that one tactic guarantees a job.

## Working Thesis

Getting a first job has become harder because four systems overlap. Some visible listings are not immediate hiring opportunities. AI is reshaping a limited set of beginner tasks in highly exposed occupations. Some employers, especially in technology, are asking for more prior experience. At the same time, the wider US market is hiring fewer people even while employment remains fairly stable. The result is a missing-first-rung problem: applicants need work to prove they can work, while employers reduce risk by favoring people who have already done it.

## Research Questions

1. How can a job opening be visible without representing an immediate external hire?
2. Is AI already reducing entry-level employment, or is the evidence narrower and less certain?
3. Where has the experience bar measurably moved upward?
4. How can employment grow while it still feels unusually difficult to enter a new job?
5. What can a first-time applicant realistically identify or improve without pretending the market is fair?
6. Which facts can become simple visual props for a B1 English audience?

## What People Think

- **Example:** `There are millions of openings, so I must be applying badly.`
- **Example:** `AI deleted every beginner job.`
- **Example:** `Entry-level has always meant zero experience.`
- **Example:** `If unemployment is not exploding, hiring must be healthy.`
- **Inference:** These beliefs turn a system problem into private shame because applicants can see their own rejection but cannot see canceled requisitions, internal candidates, hiring budgets, or firm-level risk rules.

## What Is Actually Happening

1. **Fact - some listings are weak signals:** A public listing can represent an evergreen pipeline, an internal-candidate process, a canceled role, a duplicated ad, or an out-of-date posting. Intentional fake posting exists as an allegation and policy concern, but its prevalence is not established by one reliable universal percentage.
2. **Fact - AI evidence is real but narrow:** Stanford found a notable relative decline for workers ages 22-25 in highly exposed occupations, especially where AI can automate tasks. Yale found no clear economy-wide disruption, and a New York Fed survey found both reduced and increased hiring among AI users.
3. **Fact - the experience shift is clearest in tech:** Junior tech postings have fallen more than senior postings, and the share of experience-stating tech ads asking for 5+ years has increased. This is not evidence that every entry-level occupation moved the same way.
4. **Fact - the market can be stable and difficult to enter:** A 3.2% US hires rate means fewer new matches. Low separations can keep employment growing even while outsiders struggle to get in.
5. **Inference:** All four forces make employers' visible demand look larger or friendlier than their actual willingness to train an unproven applicant.

## Why It Keeps Happening

1. **Fact:** Employers use previous work, especially relevant internships, as a tie-breaker between otherwise similar candidates.
2. **Inference:** Experience acts like a risk receipt. It tells an employer that someone else already paid part of the training cost.
3. **Inference:** In a low-hire market, firms have less pressure to accept uncertainty. They can wait longer, freeze a role, or favor an applicant who needs less training.
4. **Inference:** When AI absorbs a simple task, the task does not always disappear completely. It may become review, judgment, or tool-supervision work, which is harder to assign to a complete beginner.
5. **Inference:** Applicants respond by sending more applications. Recruiters face more volume, feedback gets worse, and job seekers receive even less information about which rung was real.

Net loop: weak or raised opening -> more applications -> less feedback -> more applicant guessing -> employers lean harder on simple experience filters.

## Safe Claims

- **Fact:** The US hires rate was 3.2% in April 2026, near its weakest level since 2013.
- **Fact:** In US tech postings that stated experience, the share asking for 5+ years rose from 37% to 42% between Q2 2022 and Q2 2025.
- **Fact:** Junior or standard tech postings were further below the February 2020 level than senior or manager postings in the Indeed analysis.
- **Fact:** Stanford found a 16% relative employment decline for ages 22-25 in the most AI-exposed occupations after firm-level controls.
- **Fact:** Yale did not find discernible economy-wide labor disruption attributable to AI in its broad measures.
- **Fact:** Some public listings remain visible because of evergreen hiring, talent pooling, internal candidates, pauses, cancellations, duplication, or neglect.
- **Inference:** These forces make the first rung harder to identify and reach, especially for applicants without a prior work signal.
- **Example:** A fictional entry-level listing asking for three years can illustrate the contradiction, provided it is labeled or presented as a generic example.

## Claims To Avoid

- `Sixteen percent of all entry-level jobs disappeared because of AI.` The Stanford result is a relative change in selected occupations and does not establish causation.
- `AI is the main reason young people cannot find jobs.` The source set does not establish that ranking.
- `Twenty percent of all listings are ghost jobs.` Definitions and methods vary, and unfilled does not automatically mean fake.
- `The 2.2 million gap between openings and hires counts ghost jobs.` Openings are a stock and hires are a monthly flow.
- `Every entry-level job now asks for five years.` The strongest tightening evidence here is tech-specific.
- `Stable employment means the job market is healthy for new applicants.` Low hires and low separations can coexist.
- `Internships guarantee jobs.` NACE measured influence between otherwise equal candidates.
- Any claim that blames individual recruiters, one generation, immigrants, or applicants as a group.

## Research Decision

The four-reason structure is strong enough to move into script, but each reason needs a different confidence level. Reason 4, the low-hire market, has the cleanest current macro evidence. Reason 3 is also strong when explicitly scoped to technology and employer preference for demonstrated experience. Reason 2 is compelling only as an early, occupation-specific AI signal paired with the Yale counterweight. Reason 1 is visually strong and structurally real, but the pack should explain types of weak listings rather than claim a universal ghost-job percentage.

The script's strongest hidden system is employer risk reduction. Ghost listings hide whether demand is live, AI can remove practice tasks, experience requirements favor already-tested applicants, and weak hiring lets firms wait. The ending should help viewers diagnose the barrier and show relevant proof, while clearly stating that individual tactics cannot repair a frozen market.
