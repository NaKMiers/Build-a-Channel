It is 2am and you are winning an argument that ended last Tuesday.

The line you actually said at the time was fine. Forgettable, even. But the line you have now is perfect. It is calm, it is precise, it lands exactly where it needs to land, and in your version the other person has nothing to say back. You have delivered it about fourteen times tonight. In the shower. On the walk to the station. Standing in front of the open fridge at midnight with the cold air on your face, staring at nothing, your mouth moving very slightly.

The argument itself lasted four minutes. You have now spent six days on it.

And the strangest part is that you are not really angry anymore. If someone stopped you and asked what the fight was actually about, you would struggle to give a clean answer. But some machine at the back of your head keeps threading the tape back to the start, and it does not seem to care how many times you tell it to stop.

You have probably decided this means you are petty. That you hold grudges. That you overthink everything and always have.

None of that is what is happening. What is happening is that your brain has a file open, and nobody ever taught it how to close one.

Begin with the oldest clue, because it comes from a restaurant.

The story goes that the psychologist Kurt Lewin was sitting with his students in a busy restaurant and noticed something odd about the waiter. The man could recall a complicated unpaid order in perfect detail, item by item, table by table. But once the bill was settled, the order evaporated from his memory almost instantly. Paid meant gone. One of Lewin's students, Bluma Zeigarnik, took that observation into a laboratory in 1927 and tested it properly. She gave people simple tasks, puzzles, small constructions, arithmetic, and let them finish about half of them. The other half she interrupted partway through.

Afterwards she asked them what they had done. People remembered the interrupted tasks roughly twice as well as the ones they had completed.
