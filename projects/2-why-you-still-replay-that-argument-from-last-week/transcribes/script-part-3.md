So small societies built machinery for exactly that, and some of it is astonishing.

E. Adamson Hoebel, who spent years documenting how law works in societies without courts, described the song duels of the Greenlandic Inuit. Two people with a grievance do not fight. They compose songs about each other. Then, in front of the assembled community, they perform them, taking turns, trading verses of mockery and complaint, sometimes for a long time, sometimes across more than one gathering. The audience listens and the audience laughs. Whoever draws the laughter wins.

And then it is over. Not metaphorically over. Formally over. The matter has been heard by everybody who mattered, a result has been produced in public, and the two are expected to carry on as members of the same community. Hoebel recorded that former opponents frequently resumed ordinary relations afterwards.

Look at what that ritual actually delivers, because it is not entertainment. It gives the dispute an audience, a hearing, a verdict, and above all an ending. A visible, dated, witnessed ending that both parties and everyone around them can point to.

That is what your nervous system is built to expect. Not victory. Closure, in front of witnesses, on a short clock.

Now come back to last Tuesday.

The argument happened in a meeting room, or over text, or in a car with the engine still running. There was no audience, or there was an audience that pretended not to hear. Nobody adjudicated. The other person walked out, or you did, or the thread just stopped being replied to. Then the modern miracle happened: you went home and you did not have to see them. You could mute them, avoid that corridor, decline the invitation, let it fade. Ancient conflict ended in a ceremony. Modern conflict ends in a slow disconnection, which is not an ending at all.

Your brain never received the signal. No verdict was announced, no witnesses confirmed anything, and the person is still out there somewhere holding their version of you. As far as that ancient system is concerned, a live grievance is sitting unresolved inside your band and nobody has said the matter is closed. So it keeps the file open, exactly as designed, and it brings it to you when the room finally goes quiet at 2am.

Notice who is always in the replay.

There is always an audience, isn't there? The version you rehearse in the shower is never just you and them in an empty room. There are people watching. Colleagues, friends, someone whose opinion you care about, sometimes a vague jury with no faces at all. And in the perfect version, they hear your line, and they see it, and something passes across the room that says clearly and finally: you were right about this.

That is not pettiness. That is a song duel with no community to hold it. You are running two-hundred-thousand-year-old dispute software, and the software requires witnesses, so your brain is supplying imaginary ones every single night, waiting for a verdict that will never be issued because there is nobody there to issue it.

Which is where the shift is, and it is not what you would expect. It is not forgive them, and it is not go and have the conversation, and it is definitely not stop thinking about it, which has never worked for any human being ever.

The psychologists E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister followed the Zeigarnik trail to its most useful destination. They confirmed that unfinished goals push their way into your thinking and degrade your attention on everything else. Then they added one step. They had people make a specific plan for the unfinished task. Not do it. Not complete it. Just decide, concretely, what would happen and when.

The intrusive thoughts dropped away. The mind released the task as though it had already been handled.

So the loop does not need a win. It needs a decision. One sentence, said out loud in the car or written on your phone, that names what you are going to do about last Tuesday and when. I am going to raise it with her on Thursday and say the one thing I actually meant. Or, I am going to let this go and never bring it up, and if it happens again, that is when I speak. Both of those close the file. What keeps it open is the state you are in right now, which is undecided, endlessly gathering evidence for a trial that has not been scheduled.

That is the whole change. Stop trying to win it. Give it an ending, and give the ending a date.

And when the tape starts again anyway, because it will for a few nights, you do not have to argue with it. You just answer it with the plan. That is already decided. That is Thursday. The system asking the question is not stupid and it is not out to get you. It is doing precisely the job it was built for, in a world that removed the fire, the audience, and the laughter that used to end things.

You are not petty. You are not fragile. You are carrying a dispute-resolution instinct calibrated for thirty people who could not walk away from each other, and running it in a world where everyone can walk away from everyone, at any time, without a word.

It is 2am and you are winning an argument that ended last Tuesday, because you were never actually trying to win it. You were waiting for someone to say it was finished. Nobody is coming to say that. So you say it, you name what happens next, and the file finally closes.
