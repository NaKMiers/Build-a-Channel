Think about what that means. Finishing something does not just end the work. It ends the remembering. An unfinished task stays loaded, live, taking up room, and it keeps quietly announcing itself until it is resolved. Your mind is not a filing cabinet where everything sits equally still. It is a desk, and the unfinished items are the ones left open in front of you, glowing.

So what is an argument, in those terms?

It is the most unfinished thing a human being can be handed. A puzzle you did not solve is merely incomplete. An argument is incomplete and contested. Two people walked away holding different accounts of what just happened, both of them certain, and no result was ever declared. Nobody said the matter is settled. Nobody said you were right, or they were right, or that it did not matter. The tab was never paid. So the waiter in your head keeps the order memorised.

Which explains why it stays. It does not yet explain why it stays like this, in loops, at 2am.

For that, the work of the psychologist Susan Nolen-Hoeksema is unavoidable. She spent her career on the difference between thinking about a problem and ruminating on one, and the difference turns out to be enormous. Rumination is repetitive, passive, circular attention to a distressing event and to how bad it feels. Crucially, it does not feel like a malfunction from the inside. It feels productive. It feels like you are getting to the bottom of something, figuring out what they really meant, working out what you should have said, preparing yourself so this never happens again.

Nolen-Hoeksema found the opposite. In her studies, people put into a ruminative state generated worse solutions to their own interpersonal problems than people who were not, while feeling more certain they were solving them. Rumination keeps a low mood running for longer, narrows what you can see, and shrinks the odds you will actually do anything about the situation. The tape feels like problem-solving. It is the thing standing in the place where the problem-solving would go.

And then there is the piece almost nobody wants to hear.

There is a folk theory, very widespread, that this stuff has to come out. That you have to let it burn through, get it off your chest, vent it, and once the pressure is released you will be calm. The psychologist Brad Bushman tested this about as directly as it can be tested. He deliberately angered participants, then had one group hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who had angered them. Pure catharsis, exactly as prescribed. Then he measured how aggressive they were afterwards.

They were more aggressive. Not less. The people who had vented were angrier at the person, and more willing to lash out at bystanders who had done nothing at all.

Every replay is not a release. It is a repetition. You are not draining the argument out of yourself, you are rehearsing it, and the brain is very good at learning whatever you rehearse. The fourteenth performance in front of the fridge does not empty the tank. It grooves the track a little deeper.

So why would evolution install a system that punishes you for six days over four minutes?

Because for almost the entire existence of your species, four minutes of conflict was genuinely dangerous, and six days was roughly how long you had to fix it.

Picture the actual conditions. A band of thirty or so people. Everyone related to everyone by blood, marriage, or a debt of meat. You have known these faces since you were born and you will know them until you die. There is no other room to go to. There is no other camp within three days' walk that would take you, and if there were, you would arrive as a stranger with no kin and no standing, which in that world is not a fresh start but an exposure.

Now have an argument in there. With your brother-in-law, over a share of the kill, in front of six people who heard every word.

You cannot block him. You cannot leave the conversation and simply never see him again. You will be sitting at the same fire tonight. You will be hunting alongside him within the week and your children will be raised in the same twenty metres of ground. The anthropologist Max Gluckman described exactly this arrangement in his work on conflict in African societies, in what he called the peace in the feud. Because people are bound to each other by ties running in several directions at once, a man's opponent in one dispute is his ally, his in-law, or his creditor in another. Conflict cannot be allowed to run to the end of its own logic, because the same ties that generate the quarrel are the ties that make the group work. The dispute has to be closed, and everyone has an interest in closing it.
