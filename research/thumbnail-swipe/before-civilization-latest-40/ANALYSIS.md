# Before Civilization: content and curiosity analysis of 40 thumbnails

## Scope

This analysis covers the 40 source JPG files in this folder, ordered from newest to oldest.
The corresponding video titles come from `index.tsv`. The purpose is not to copy the channel's
characters or exact pictures. It is to understand how each thumbnail turns a video topic into
one concrete event, how the headline and scene cooperate, what answer is withheld, and why a
viewer may stop scrolling.

The two ratings are qualitative:

- Alignment measures how directly the headline and pictured event express the video's real
  content.
- Curiosity measures how strongly the combination creates a specific unanswered question.

## The central content formula

The strongest examples follow one repeatable structure:

1. The image freezes one physical event with an immediate problem.
2. The headline points at that exact event instead of naming the broad topic.
3. The image answers the headline enough to make the premise understandable.
4. The image also introduces a missing cause, choice, threat, or next step.
5. The viewer can form one precise question in less than a second.

The headline and image therefore do not carry separate hooks. They form one shared curiosity
loop. For example, `NOW WHAT?` becomes meaningful only because a dead mammoth and three puzzled
hunters are visible. The picture answers "what happened" but withholds "how can they use or move
it now?" That second question is the click.

## Cross-set findings

### 1. The best headline points to a noun or action already visible

Strong referents include:

- `it` pointing to a tiny fire
- `this` pointing to a mammoth carcass used as shelter
- `sound` represented by fleeing people and off-frame sound waves
- `next` represented by predators approaching two possible victims
- `now` represented by a completed mammoth kill with no next action

Weak headlines such as `HOW?`, `EVERY NIGHT`, and `ONE MISTAKE` without a visible mistake force
the viewer to invent the premise.

### 2. The image should reveal the setup and hide the decisive answer

The image should normally answer:

- Who is in danger?
- What physical event is happening?
- What object, animal, or resource matters?
- Why should the viewer care right now?

It should withhold at least one of:

- the source of the threat
- the next consequence
- the survival method
- the reason for a strange behavior
- the winner of a decision
- the true meaning of an object or ritual

`ONE SOUND = RUN` reveals the reaction and hides the source. `HELP ME` reveals the injury and
hides the rescue outcome. `NOW WHAT?` reveals the successful kill and hides the logistical
solution.

### 3. A frozen decision is stronger than a broad condition

`LEAVE HIM?` is stronger than a generic image of tribal hardship because the viewer can
mentally choose a side. `DON'T LET IT DIE` is stronger than `NO HEATERS` because one resource
is failing now. `NO ESCAPE` is stronger than `Too Cold?` because the wave will hit in the next
second.

The useful unit is not "topic." It is "one second before an irreversible consequence."

### 4. Shock works when it remains relevant

Useful shock is concrete evidence from the video:

- drinking blood and eating raw fat
- sleeping inside a mammoth carcass
- a crying child attracting a predator
- a group considering abandonment
- ritual smoke and altered eyes

Weak shock uses incoherent or misleading symbols, such as a modern gravestone, red arrow, or
anachronistic water pump. Shock should deepen the promise, not replace it.

### 5. The strongest questions are specific enough to predict the answer category

The viewer does not know the answer, but understands what kind of answer is coming:

- What sound caused the flight?
- How do they save the fire?
- How do they move or use the mammoth?
- Do they abandon the injured person?
- Why does the wolf stay beside sleeping humans?

`HOW?` is weak because the possible answer category is unlimited. `WHO'S NEXT` is strong because
the possible outcome is narrow and immediate.

### 6. Headline functions that repeatedly work

1. Urgent command: `DON'T LET IT DIE`
2. Hidden cause: `ONE SOUND = RUN`
3. Direct plea: `HELP ME`
4. Scarcity: `LAST COPY`, `ONLY 6 WEEKS`
5. Missing next step: `NOW WHAT?`
6. Binary moral choice: `LEAVE HIM?`
7. Reversal: `AFRAID OF US?`
8. Shocking reveal: `ATE WHAT?`, `SLEPT INSIDE THIS?!`
9. Imminent victim: `WHO'S NEXT`
10. Concrete constraint: `NO ESCAPE`, `NO FIRE?`

### 7. Visual ingredients of high-curiosity scenes

- One dominant person with an extreme readable reaction
- One physical problem that can be named in one sentence
- One supporting threat, victim, resource, or witness
- A before-or-after moment, not a neutral pose
- Evidence of what just happened
- Evidence of what may happen next
- An off-frame or partially hidden variable

The best frames often have two timelines inside one image: the completed action and the next
danger. A dead mammoth proves success, while the confused hunters reveal the new problem. A
broken leg proves injury, while predator eyes reveal the approaching consequence.

## Why the Project 3 thumbnails feel disconnected

The Project 3 video is about audience-shaped identity, the dread of incompatible friend groups
meeting, and the fact that each group holds a different but genuine version of the same person.
Its most naturally clickable event is therefore the collision of two social worlds and the
specific thing each group expects from the protagonist.

The generated concepts drift away from that core event:

- `THEY JUST MET` is the closest to the script, but the scene shows a normal introduction. It
  does not reveal what incompatible versions of the protagonist are about to collide or what
  socially dangerous detail may be exposed.
- `WHO MOVED THE NEEDLE?` requires prior knowledge of the sociometer metaphor. A gauge moving is
  not an instinctively meaningful threat to a new viewer.
- `EVERYONE WAS WATCHING` illustrates the ancestral reputation section, but a viewer cannot
  infer the modern friend-group problem from it.
- `ONE CAMP. SEVEN ROOMS.` explains the thesis as a comparison. It behaves like an infographic,
  not an unresolved event.
- `DON'T MERGE THEM` visualizes self-complexity as a ship bulkhead, but the metaphor is several
  reasoning steps away from friend groups and identity.

The visual polish is strong, but the concepts ask the viewer to decode the theory before feeling
the stakes. Before Civilization does the opposite: it makes the stakes physical first, then
lets the video explain the theory.

## Content standard to carry into future thumbnail concepts

Before writing a prompt, require one sentence in this form:

`A viewer sees [specific person] doing [specific action] because [visible problem], one second
before [implied consequence], and asks [one precise unanswered question].`

Reject the concept if:

- the scene only summarizes the topic
- the headline could fit ten unrelated videos
- understanding the danger requires knowing a psychological term or metaphor
- the image answers the headline completely without creating a second question
- the main event is not from the script
- the consequence is neither visible nor strongly implied
- the viewer cannot state the curiosity question in one short sentence

For Project 3 specifically, future concepts should stay close to a script-native social event:
two groups meeting, one version of the protagonist being exposed to the other, one private joke
or true detail crossing a boundary, or one audience reacting to behavior that belongs to another
audience. The scene should make the collision socially dangerous before any theory, meter, camp,
room count, or ship metaphor is introduced.
